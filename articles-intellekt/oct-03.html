Can a country be truly democratic if some people are far richer than others?

25|10|2026

There is a paradox nested within the crux of this question. Democracy, once interpreted, essentially refers to equality. However, the concept of currency — at least in the way it is used to structure society now — may extinguish the very premise of this concept.

Karl Marx already illuminated part of this claim in The Communist Manifesto where he introduces how society is divided between two classes: the bourgeoisie and the proletariat. He emphasises how the capitalist economy is the heart of this inequality; however, what he overlooks is the true epicentre of this problem, to which the finger can only point toward money.

Trace currency back to its origins and one would notice that it was essentially meritocracy: the farmer could work harder on the field to get more in return. However, when this framework evolves and spreads globally, the assumption underpinning this structure becomes increasingly more unstable. When the rich continue to transfer their wealth generationally, it creates a widening gap between the starting points of the rest of society as opposed to the wealthy; where the rich get the superior education, job, and opportunity which puts the subsequent generation even farther ahead. Essentially, money becomes valuable only because it widens this gap and one can inherit access to opportunity. If everybody in a society possessed the same amount of money, the rich would be stripped of the label, and money — after a certain threshold — would be stripped of its value because it is not enabling elitism in an equal society.

Money gives power because money gives land, security, and opportunity; and power can only exist relative to another and therefore, in a hierarchy. If the citizen had as much power as the state, what power would the state really behold? If money can buy power, then money cannot coexist with equality.

Having said that, two distinct contradictions emerge: firstly, if power bought through money and equality cannot coexist, how do horizontal power structures in socialist societies exist whilst still preserving the notion of money; and secondly, in most modern democracies, individuals do have equal rights and still retain the concept of currency.

Responding to the latter: if a modern democracy is investigated, there is an apparent distinction needed to be made. Every citizen constitutionally has equal fundamental rights — such as every adult having the right to vote — one could argue that this is the only mandate to classify a democracy; however, does democracy stop at equal voting or at societal equality? Democracy is derived from the Greek word “demokratia” which can directly be translated into “rule by the people” and if discerned fundamentally, perhaps one could argue that equal voting is the only requirement. However, if analysed, it refers to “people” as one group; undivided. Obviously, a democracy with different candidates would suggest division, but class division would entail division on a non-candidate basis which introduces another aspect in judgement excluding the sole competence of a particular candidate; it influences the movement of society toward a direction or interest that is not aligned with what is actually best for it. Thereby, nourishing the stomach of unfairness. Therefore, if a society seems divided on the grounds of class and actively acts as a determinant aside from sole competence, it is not a proper democracy.

Returning to the discussion, socialism produces a society where money is preserved whilst every citizen is equal without class divide, now would this be truly equal? I contend no, for the reason that humans, by nature, will assign value to whatever may give power in order to serve the same purpose as money. For example, in the Soviet Union, wages for each citizen were flat, and prices were fixed. Citizens instead, focused value on items — such as western denim or alcohol — and used a system known informally as “blat” which acted as value through favours or social connections. For example, a friend working in the meat-packing plant would be of instant value because instead of having to wait for fresh meat and having to wait in line for it, one could have direct access. Hierarchy started developing through different means but achieved the same thing money is: creating a structure of power and privilege.

The indelible truth that humanity stands under is that a society cannot exist without distinguishing the powerless from the powerful— achieved through whatever means possible because nobody is born inherently powerful, and society compensates by assigning value to a resource or position to enable that structure. Humanity can move in two directions: a civilisation unlike any preceding it with no value assigned to anything, or continuing to walk along the path it is on now and accept the unfairness that arrives with it. This is the dilemma marking the mountain that humanity stands over. Where one edge leads to another mountain to climb, and the other leads to falling through the ever-widening trench of inequality.
