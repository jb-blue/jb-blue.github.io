Formulise

21|11|2026

Last week, I went to the finals of a school debate and it was really tasty intellectual chewing material that I’d wish to share to everybody’s plates. To provide context, both sides of the debate consisted of individuals in the eighth grade and it seemed to be conducted properly in the WSDC format as well as judged reasonably. The motion stated was “this house, as a child, would sue their parents for religious indoctrination.” The context provided was essentially describing a theocratic society wherein a child is compelled to seek legal protection on the grounds of religious indoctrination, defined as coercively imposing religious beliefs through manipulation, threats, or the suppression of alternative perspectives. I would try, to the best of my ability, make the least number of remarks I can arguing or participating in the debate, as my primary goal is to comment on the style of the debate as well as assess which angle was chosen to argue upon on either side. The Prime Minister began by criticising how opposition’s worldview is too idealistic with the analogy of an Instagram feed which is relevant, to some extent, though she fails to connect it to her main argument, that agency is important — which seems to be loosely tied into her unstructured monologue — however, she also fails to express how a child choosing not to proceed legally would be stripped of agency. She talked as if she were a bullet train with its tracks broken with no apparent direction — starting and stopping rapidly that it was understandable but distracting; effectively, a nervous wreck. This was most noticeable in the middle of her speech where she tried to explain a chain of logic that I couldn’t follow after a certain point. “But if my parents tell me repeatedly that if I don’t follow Neevism, the whole Asteria will look down at me; they won’t allow me to make society better. They won’t make me a part of all this. How would that make me feel? That would make me feel horrible, right? Because I would want to fit into the society. I would not want to be left out. I would not want to be thrown away I would not want to be not looked upon, right? I would want to be part of this society. I would want to be culminated in it, right? Which means that these children lose agency, right? And they won’t be able to speak up and they won’t be able to do what they want to do, right? What they want to believe. And opp(osition) will come and say that this fear exists on both sides, right? The fear is what will make these children not go and follow these legal protections and this fear is what will make them not want to go up and speak against their parents and hence, the whole use of this legal protection in the first place, is useless.” Then, she proceeds to rebut by repeating the same point about how the fear caused by the society and their parents is immense and therefore, agency should be given to combat it; though, the only thought occupying my mind was why would the children be motivated to publicly announce their religious identity when a) the fear is already so intense and an active restriction when considering legal processes, b) the fact that it would result in ostracisation — which is exactly what the Prime Minister claims she doesn’t want! And c) that religious indoctrination would persist or perhaps even amplify through the literal theocratic society coercing them, if not the parents. The final point was actually a point of interest raised by opposition and it was responded to by saying the equivalent of “screw society, they will always say something.” Dare I say, she spoke as if she was terrified of her own stance. Now whether the stakes would warrant that level of stress is not what I’m going to discuss, but relatively, it was the elephant in the room for her speech. Next, the Leader of Opposition began, and I can’t stress this enough, her speech felt like a sip of cold water after crossing the Sahara Desert. She started with a sentimental image but she didn’t linger, and went straight to her point, which was how much emotional distress it would cause the individuals who might not be capable of managing it properly. That wasn’t necessarily a bad argument, but subsequently she brought out the crux of the motion that proposition couldn’t understand in the entire debate. “Panel, this debate is not about whether people should have this agency to go ahead and do this. It’s about whether doing this is actually the best way for people to go ahead and opt out of whatever religious coercion that’s being forced upon them.” Now I don’t have much to say about her speech, aside from the fact that it actually had some structure and a chain of logic that I could follow; though, it’s far from flawless, she — alongside almost every other debater — reinstated the same point over and over again to fulfil a time quota. I’d bore both you, and myself if I continued to talk about every speakers speech in the debate, however there were a few details that stood out: first — I don’t know whether this is a debating instinct — but hand gestures were used to a great extent that it was almost comedic trying to listen to their speech when oppositions whip looked like she was throwing gang signs and proposition’s whip looked like he wanted to jump and did Bharatnatyam I think belongs in Malleshwaram, not a debate; secondly, the openings each speaker used were suspicious. Undoubtedly, they were excellent and engaging — such as proposition’s whip using his last situationship to highlight the lack of accountability and transparency on opposition’s side of the house, and opposition’s whip stating that proposition’s argument was analogous to a Jenga tower that collapses after one assumption is challenged — those openings sounded oddly repeated and scripted; almost like a formality like “regards” at the end of an email, but instead of seeking acceptability, it sounded like part of a formula given to debaters to catch a win. I’m not accusing the debaters of being lackluster in debating skill or prowess, though the pattern of all debaters seeming to seek a garrulous manner of speaking combined with recalling and using the most appropriate opening from the supposed list that was given to them was undeniably present. There’s this common denominator in anything competitive, where after something — like debate — gets popular enough, aspects of it get formula-ised (I’ll personally like to use “formulised”) or, in a way, optimised for said achievement; like a win. This phenomenon can be observed in anything, but most notably in the college application fear wave caused by influencers — often claiming to have some ethos — dispensing content along the lines of “here’s how to get into the Ivy League as an average high school student,” or “here’s how to describe each one of your activities to stand out to top colleges.” Now the question: is this necessarily bad? On the positive side, it provides some sort of guidance to individuals that would otherwise, be in distress if they don’t have some infrastructure to rely on. Don’t misunderstand: I think structure and some boundaries is necessary to any organised system functioning; but at what point does this tip over to relying on how well said person can follow a method as opposed to how well they can demonstrate their skill? Of course, the debate I watched was nowhere near following some homogeneous method, though following the trajectory of the observations, that future is no longer implausible. One could argue that this does produce positive outcome as it encourages people to use a structure to debate “better”; though, through my eyes, part of what defines a skill is not merely the ability to execute it appropriately, but to be able to individually derive what and why something within that skill is strong, and giving people a formula is essentially a shortcut. Part of what made Socrates such a great thinker was the fact that that he could produce such intellectual work with essentially nothing given out to him; he developed his philosophies starting from scratch and we can read his work now, understand it, but it’s not that great of an achievement because it wasn’t taught to him — he derived it. Drawing the parallel back to the modern world, that is exactly why a skill in a particular field also relies on whether you had the capacity to understand or create something great. As always, I am open to disagreement; my truth may not be the whole truth, I’d love to hear from you.
